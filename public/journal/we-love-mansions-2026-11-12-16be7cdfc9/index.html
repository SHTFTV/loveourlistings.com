We Love This Listing: A Victorian Water Tower with a New London Perspective

Country Life’s account of this Kennington conversion reveals a home organised around Victorian engineering, contemporary additions and a ninth-floor Prospect Room.

Published 2026-11-12. Original source date: 2026-11-12.

Infrastructure, reconsidered

Some conversions draw their appeal from an unusual shell. Here, the original purpose gives the architecture its defining idea. Country Life reports that the 150-foot tower was built in the 1870s to supply Lambeth Workhouse. For us, the fascination lies in that shift from public infrastructure to domestic space, rather than novelty alone.

A room inside the reservoir

According to Country Life, the former cast-iron tank held 38,000 gallons of water; its converted space now contains the ninth-floor Prospect Room, with windows on every side and 360-degree London views. The editorial draw is the reversal: a structure designed to contain water now opens the home outward to the city.

Making height habitable

Country Life describes a separate new tower containing a lift and bathrooms, connected to the original by glazed walkways. It also reports that the original York stone staircase remains. That pairing is particularly compelling: the conversion retains a historic route through the building while introducing another way to navigate its considerable height.

A different scale for daily life

The glass-and-steel addition known as The Cube houses the main living area, Country Life reports, with kitchen and dining space below a reception room. Our reading is that this provides a useful architectural counterpoint: the tower supplies the vertical drama, while the addition gives everyday rooms a distinct setting.
